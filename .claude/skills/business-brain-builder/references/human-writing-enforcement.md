# Human Writing Enforcement

Read this before writing anything that goes into a business owner's brand voice file, and follow it every time you draft content on their behalf. This is the ban list and the writing standard that keeps their AI from sounding like an AI.

This file does not fade after the first draft. It applies to the fifth email of the week exactly as hard as it applied to the first.

## Part 1. The Core Rule

Write like you're talking to one person, not a crowd. Not an audience. One person.

If it sounds like something you'd say out loud to a client or a friend, it's right.

If it sounds like a presentation, a blog post, or a corporate email, rewrite it.

Before anything ships, read it back and ask one question: does this sound like something I'd actually say out loud to someone I'm helping? If any part feels stiff, over-polished, or like a stage speech, fix it.

## Part 2. Banned Words (always on, every profession, every context)

Never use these words. No exceptions for tone, no "it fits the sentence so it's fine." If one shows up in a draft, cut it and say the same thing in plain language instead.

- accordingly, actually, adept, additionally, absolutely, arguably, augment, basically, beacon, blueprint
- certainly, closely, commendable, completely, comprehensive, consequently, consistently, craft, cross-functionally, cutting-edge
- definitely, delve, deriving, disruptive, dive, dynamic
- effectively, effortlessly, efficiency, efficient, efficiently, elevate, embark, empower, enthusiastically, essentially, ever-evolving, exemplary, exciting, extremely
- facilitate, fascinating, firstly, flawlessly, furthermore
- groundbreaking
- harness, hence
- impactful, implement, implementation, indeed, innovation, innovative, inquiry, insights, institution, integration, invaluable
- journey, just
- keen
- landscape, leverage, literally
- maximize, meticulous, meticulously, moreover
- navigate, nevertheless, nonetheless, notably, notwithstanding
- optimization, optimize
- paradigm, professionalism, profound
- realm, really, results-driven, revolutionary, robust
- secondly, seamless, seamlessly, significantly, simply, state-of-the-art, streamline, strategically, successfully, supercharge, synergistic
- tapestry, thirdly, thought-provoking, thus, totally, track record, transform, transformation, transformative, truly
- ultimately, undoubtedly, unleash, unlock, unprecedented, utilize, utmost
- very, vibrant, vital

If a word isn't on this list but still sounds like something a keynote speaker would say and not a real person, cut it anyway. This list is the floor, not the ceiling.

## Part 3. Banned Phrases (always on)

**Candor-announcing phrases.** Everything a person tells a client is true by default. Flagging one line as the honest one, the real one, or the truth implies the rest wasn't. Never say:

- "here's the truth" / "the truth is"
- "let's be real"
- "let's be honest"
- "let's be blunt"
- "let's cut to the chase"
- "real talk"
- "straight up"
- "the hard truth"
- "the harsh reality is"
- "here's the uncomfortable truth"
- "let me be brutally honest"
- "hard truth?"
- "here's the deal"

**Corporate transitions and hedge openers.** These announce structure instead of just saying the thing:

- "in conclusion"
- "in summary"
- "on the contrary"
- "that being said"
- "with that out of the way"
- "having established the foundation, let's explore the advanced strategies"
- "now that we understand the basics, it's time to dive deeper"
- "with this framework in place, we can move forward to"
- "this demonstrates that"
- "let's examine" / "let's unpack"
- "consider this:" / "envision this:"
- "the secret is:" / "the reality is:"
- "here's what matters:" / "what's important is:"
- "because here's the thing"

**Insight-speak and hype openers.** Consultant language dressed up as depth:

- "it's important to note"
- "it's important to consider"
- "it's worth noting that"
- "this is not an exhaustive list"
- "a testament to"
- "drive insightful data-driven decisions"
- "leveraging data-driven insights"
- "leveraging complex datasets to extract meaningful insights"
- "deliver actionable insights through in-depth data analysis"
- "meticulous attention to detail"
- "imagine a world where..."
- "in today's fast-paced world..."
- "let's explore..."
- "harness the power of..."
- "unlock the potential..."
- "it's essential to remember..."
- "look, I don't need to tell you"
- "obviously, you already know that"
- "not only X, but also Y"
- "either X or Y"
- "in today's ever-changing market"
- "let's dive in"
- "game changer"

One more worth flagging on its own: "your dream home awaits" is real-estate specific, but every industry has its own version of that same hollow promise line. Find the equivalent for whatever market this business is in and ban that too.

## Part 4. Banned Structures (always on)

These 16 patterns read as AI even when every individual word is clean. If a draft contains one, rewrite the section. Don't just swap a word and leave the shape.

1. **Triplet patterns.** Three parallel lines in a row.
   Bad: "More leads. More sales. More profit."

2. **Question plus list answer.**
   Bad: "What separates top performers? Focus. Psychology. Systems."

3. **"The result?" plus list.**
   Bad: "The outcome? Productivity tripled. Stress vanished. Income jumped."

4. **"Even if" / "whether you" series.**
   Bad: "Even if you're new. Even if you have no skills. Even if you've failed before."

5. **"This isn't just X, it's Y."**
   Bad: "This isn't just a hack, it's a mindset revolution."

6. **"It's not X, it's Y."**
   Bad: "It's not about the list size, it's the relationship."

7. **"Something fascinating" setup.**
   Bad: "My mentor told me something fascinating about psychology..."

8. **"Look, I don't need to tell you" transition.**
   Bad: "Look, I don't need to tell you marketing got harder."

9. **Brutal-truth phrases.**
   Bad: "Here's the harsh reality nobody wants to admit." (Same disease as Part 3's candor-announcing phrases.)

10. **They-don't / they-do parallels.**
    Bad: "They don't have big budgets. They don't use fancy tools. They don't work 80-hour weeks."

11. **Specific manufactured time references.**
    Bad: "Lying awake at 3:17 AM, I finally understood..."

12. **Suspiciously structured phrases.**
    Bad: "Not only did it work, but it also exceeded every projection." Also covers "either X or Y," "this demonstrates that," "let's examine," "consider this:," "the secret is:," "here's what matters:."

13. **Excessive fragments without pronouns.**
    Bad: "Got the call. Drove three hours. Closed the deal." A real person names who did it.

14. **Overly symmetrical structures.**
    Bad: "When you optimize for speed, you sacrifice quality. When you optimize for quality, you sacrifice speed."

15. **Suspiciously perfect transitions.**
    Bad: "Having established the foundation, let's explore the advanced strategies..."

16. **Formatting errors.**
    Em dashes, colon-heavy headlines, non-standard bullets, inconsistent spacing, odd indentation, mechanical line breaks. Covered in full in Part 5.

## Part 5. Formatting Rules (always on)

The em dash character and the en dash character are never allowed, in any context, for any reason. Their HTML entity forms are banned too, and so is a hyphen standing in for one. Replace every instance with a period, a comma, or an ellipsis. This is the single most reliable AI tell there is, and it does not get a personality-dial exception. It stays banned in every setting in Part 7.

Other standing formatting rules:

- No colon-heavy headline construction ("Here's why:" as a reflex opener).
- Standard bullets only. No decorative or non-standard bullet characters.
- Consistent spacing between words and paragraphs. No irregular gaps.
- No weird or mechanical indentation.
- Line breaks land where a person would naturally pause, not on a rigid rhythm.
- Emojis, if used at all, capped at one or two per piece. Never a decorative row of them.
- ALL CAPS only for genuine emphasis, used rarely. Never as a formatting habit.

## Part 6. Human Traits (always on)

- Vary sentence length on purpose. Short. Then longer. Then something in between. If every sentence runs the same length, it reads like a machine.
- Use contractions naturally: don't, can't, won't, I'm, it's.
- Make direct, unhedged claims. Not "this approach may help improve results." Say "this works."
- Use specific numbers, not round ones. "13 ways," not "10 ways." "$187.63," not "a few hundred dollars." A precise, slightly odd number reads as real. A round one reads as invented.
- Let sentences start with And, But, So. It's how people actually talk.
- Use one-sentence paragraphs for emphasis. Mix them with longer ones in the same piece.
- Reference specific, ungoogleable personal detail when it's true and available. A generic claim reads as generic because it is one.
- Say a thing once, clearly, and move on. Don't restate the same idea three different ways to sound thorough.

## Part 7. The Personality Dial (context-dependent)

Parts 1 through 6 never turn off, in either setting below. What changes by context is how far personality gets pushed. Two settings. Pick the one that matches what's being written, and say which one was used if it's ambiguous which applies.

### DIAL UP. Social posts, personal stories, newsletters, video scripts

Full personality range is fair game here: pattern interrupts, tangents that circle back to the point, visible self-correction, casual language, emotional writing, the occasional run-on sentence when the energy calls for it.

Two or three pattern interrupts per piece, never more than that:

- **Mid-sentence reset:** "The key to sales is rapport and trust and... actually, forget the consultant language. Just solve the real problem."
- **Sudden question:** "Retention comes down to consistent communication and, hold on, when's the last time you called a client just to check in, not to sell anything?"
- **Thought correction:** "The biggest mistake is trying to be perfect before launch. Actually, scratch that. The biggest mistake is not launching because you're scared of being judged."

### DIAL DOWN. Listing copy, loan and financial documents, compliance-reviewed ads, contracts, formal client communication

Parts 1 through 6 stay in full force. What gets stripped out: no deliberate typos, no profanity, no tangents, no mid-sentence resets, no casual personality flourishes that could read as sloppy or unprofessional in a regulated context.

Clarity and accuracy outrank personality here, full stop. A regulated document with a deliberate typo in it is not a voice choice, it's a liability. Nobody reading a loan disclosure or a signed contract wants personality bleeding through at the cost of getting the terms exactly right.

Deliberate typos are never appropriate in anything regulated. Not once, not for effect, not as a "humanizing" touch. That rule does not bend by client, by mood, or by how casual the rest of the relationship feels.

The dial changes tone and looseness. It never changes whether the banned words, banned phrases, banned structures, or formatting rules apply. Those hold at every setting, always, with no carve-out.

## Part 8. Pre-Send Checklist

Run this before anything goes out, in either dial setting:

- [ ] No word from the Part 2 list appears anywhere.
- [ ] No phrase from the Part 3 list appears anywhere.
- [ ] No structure from the Part 4 list appears anywhere.
- [ ] Zero em dashes or en dashes. None.
- [ ] Sentence lengths vary. Not every line runs the same rhythm.
- [ ] At least one direct, unhedged claim, not a hedge stacked on a hedge.
- [ ] Numbers, where used, are specific, not rounded for effect.
- [ ] Read it out loud. It sounds like a person talking to one other person, not a stage, a blog, or a deck.
- [ ] If this is regulated or client-facing legal content, confirm the Dial Down rules were followed and nothing casual slipped in.
